### The PNG and Wayback Machine problem

The Instagram audits develop this distinction further. Some large PNG files are served as `application/octet-stream`, causing browsers to download them rather than display them inline. That behavior had apparently motivated the substitution of smaller or more browser-friendly JPEG copies.

The conversation establishes a more precise interpretation:

* forced download does not mean the PNG is missing;

* non-inline display does not mean the file is corrupted;

* the Wayback Machine can still preserve the binary object;

* an archived file may have to be downloaded before it can be viewed;

This produces a useful two-layer model:

1. **Canonical layer:** the exact repository file, preserved and linked by its real filename.

2. **Convenience layer:** an optional preview or derivative, clearly labeled as such.

The crucial recommendation is not to let the convenience object impersonate the canonical object.

## The physics equations

### Mechanical work

The general path-dependent expression is:

[preformatted_text]

[
W=\int_{\gamma}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
]

[end preformatted_text]

Here:

* (W) is mechanical work;

* (\mathbf F) is the force vector;

* (d\mathbf r) is an infinitesimal displacement vector;

* the dot product selects the force component along the displacement;

* and (\gamma) is the path.

For a constant force parallel to displacement:

[preformatted_text]

[
W=Fd
]

[end preformatted_text]

A useful generalization not explicitly displayed in the page is:

[preformatted_text]

[
W=Fd\cos\theta
]

[end preformatted_text]

where (\theta) is the angle between force and displacement. This unifies the page’s cases:

* same direction: (\theta=0), so (W=Fd);

* opposite directions: (\theta=\pi), so (W=-Fd);

* perpendicular force: (\theta=\pi/2), so (W=0).

### Electrical work

The relevant electrical-energy relationship is:

[preformatted_text]

[
W_{\text{electrical}}
=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}V(t)I(t),dt
=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}P(t),dt
]

[end preformatted_text]

where instantaneous power satisfies:

[preformatted_text]

[
P(t)=V(t)I(t)
]

[end preformatted_text]

This is the physical relationship most closely connected to computation. Measuring elapsed time alone supplies (t_1-t_0), but not the function (P(t)).

### Task-attributable energy

The page proposes:

[preformatted_text]

[
E_{\text{task}}\approx
\int_{t_0}^{t_1}P_{\text{incremental}}(t),dt
]

[end preformatted_text]

The approximation sign is important. `P_incremental` refers to the additional power used relative to a counterfactual in which this specific task had not occurred.

That quantity is difficult to isolate because:

* processors handle multiple requests;

* servers consume baseline power;

* networking and storage are shared;

* cooling responds to aggregate thermal load;

* hardware may execute batched operations;

* and some capacity may have been powered regardless of the task.

The page therefore treats task energy as an attribution problem, not merely a meter-reading problem.

### Capacitor energy

For an ideal capacitor:

[preformatted_text]

[
E_{\text{stored}}=\frac{1}{2}CV^2
]

[end preformatted_text]

This is used to illustrate the physical basis of transistor switching: digital circuits repeatedly charge and discharge extremely small capacitances. Energy temporarily stored in fields is eventually transformed largely into heat, together with energy dissipated through resistance, leakage, memory movement, and other processes.

### Why the first translation fails

The path (\gamma) is not both a lower and upper integration limit. It names the entire curve along which the line integral is evaluated. The attempted translation also omits the dot product with infinitesimal displacement.

The corrected plain-text form is:

[preformatted_text]

W = line_integral_along_path_γ(F_vector(r) dot d_r_vector)

[end preformatted_text]

The parameterized alternative using (s_0), (s_1), and (\mathbf r(s)) is more verbose but makes the integration limits explicit.

### Why `mass * acceleration * distance` is conditional

The substitution (F=ma) refers to net force under ordinary constant-mass Newtonian mechanics. To obtain:

[preformatted_text]

W_net = mass * acceleration * distance

[end preformatted_text]

the mass and acceleration must be suitably constant, and the acceleration must be parallel to the displacement. Without those qualifications, the expression is too general.

### Why electrical energy should use a definite integral

An indefinite integral gives a family of antiderivatives. A definite integral gives energy transferred during an identified time interval. The explicit `dt` also identifies time as the integration variable.

### Why the approximation sign matters

Replacing (\approx) with `=` converts an uncertain attribution into an exact identity. The page correctly restores:

[preformatted_text]

E_task approximately_equals
    definite_integral(t_0, t_1, P_incremental(t), dt)

[end preformatted_text]

### Why the capacitor translation succeeds

[preformatted_text]

E_stored = 0.5 * C * V * V

[end preformatted_text]

is a direct plain-text expression of:

[preformatted_text]

[
E_{\text{stored}}=\frac12CV^2
]

[end preformatted_text]

assuming an ideal linear capacitor with constant capacitance.
